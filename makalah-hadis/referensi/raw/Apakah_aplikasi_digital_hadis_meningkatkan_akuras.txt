Digital hadith applications partially improve verification accuracy for non-experts,
but significant limitations persist.

1. Introduction

The digitization of hadith scholarship has accelerated rapidly, with mobile applications, websites, and AI-powered
tools now serving as primary access points for millions of Muslims seeking to engage with prophetic traditions (
Pascasarjana & Parepare, 2024;       Najiyah & Putriani, 2024; Hakak et al., 2020). This digital transformation raises a
critical question: do these tools actually improve the accuracy of hadith verification among non-experts, or do they
merely democratize access while introducing new epistemological risks? The literature in this corpus reveals a
nuanced picture in which digital applications enhance speed, accessibility, and certain structured verification tasks,
yet simultaneously expose users to fabricated or weak hadiths due to inadequate scholarly oversight, low hadith
literacy, and the absence of standardized validation mechanisms ( Pascasarjana & Parepare, 2024; Abdullah et al.,
2026; Adelia et al., 2025).

Several foundational studies anchor this field. Hakak et al. provide a comprehensive survey of hadith authentication
techniques, identifying both the promise and the open challenges of computational approaches (Hakak et al.,
2020). Supriyadi et al. conducted an action research study with 398 students demonstrating measurable literacy
improvements through the EHK9I application ( Supriyadi et al., 2020). Abdullah et al. empirically tested digital
takhrij tools against 120 viral hadith posts, finding high tool accuracy but significant accessibility barriers for
ordinary users (Abdullah et al., 2026). More recently, AI-powered models have demonstrated near-human accuracy
in structured tasks like chain–text separation, while general-purpose LLMs such as ChatGPT have shown
alarmingly low accuracy in hadith verification (Asgari-Bidhendi et al., 2025; Abd et al., 2026).

2. Methods
The search ran over 220 million research papers indexed in Consensus, drawing from Semantic Scholar, PubMed,
and additional academic databases. The initial query and six research groups targeting foundational context, digital
application impact, alternative terminology, contrasting perspectives, adjacent technologies, and user experience
focus yielded approximately 190 papers after machine-learned relevance filtering. Following deduplication and final
relevance ranking, 50 papers were included in this synthesis. The corpus spans qualitative descriptive studies,
systematic literature reviews, action research, computational experiments, and mixed-methods evaluations
published between 2017 and 2026.

The corpus is anchored by several pivotal contributions. Supriyadi et al. provide the strongest empirical evidence

3.2 Accuracy of Digital Takhrij Tools

Digital takhrij tools demonstrate high source-identification accuracy when used by trained researchers. Maktabah
Syamilah achieved 91.4% accuracy in identifying hadith sources from viral social media content (Abdullah et al.,
2026), and was the most-used digital tool among researchers at 79% adoption, though with only moderate
accuracy compared to printed sources (            Abd et al., 2026). The Hadith Encyclopedia and Hadith Collection
applications provide continuously improving verification features including source text, hadith rankings, sanad, and
narrator biographies (, 2023; Zulkipli et al., 2017). A Telegram-based chatbot using LangChain Retriever and GPT-4
achieved a 0.97 scoring evaluator score and 90% accuracy rating from five hadith experts (                    Herwanza et al.,
2024). However, these tools' effectiveness depends heavily on user competence: 68.3% of viral hadith posts lacked
adequate sanad information, and accessibility barriers prevented social media users from fully utilizing the tools
(Abdullah et al., 2026).

3.4 Non-Expert User Outcomes and Limitations

Action research with 398 students demonstrated that integrating the EHK9I application with structured learning
improved data literacy by 54.33%, technological literacy by 63.07%, and hadith narration literacy by 93.59% (
Supriyadi et al., 2020). Students reported that digital hadith applications made finding references easier, increased
understanding, and enabled access anytime and anywhere (Hidayah et al., 2025). However, many applications
feature hadiths without adequate scientific verification, exposing users to weak or fabricated narrations (
Pascasarjana & Parepare, 2024). Low hadith literacy among the general public means users often rely solely on
search features without understanding sanad, context, or authenticity degrees ( Pascasarjana & Parepare, 2024).
Several applications lack transparency in presenting original sources, displaying only matan excerpts without sanad
or references (    Pascasarjana & Parepare, 2024). The shift from teacher-based learning to digital platforms erodes
traditional scholarly authority and the sanad system (        Najiyah & Putriani, 2024; Syukur et al., 2026).

4. Discussion

The corpus reveals a fundamental tension: digital hadith tools enhance verification speed and accessibility but do
not uniformly improve accuracy among non-experts. The strongest evidence comes from Supriyadi et al.'s action
research, which demonstrated that structured integration of the EHK9I application with pedagogical support
produced measurable literacy gains across three dimensions (         Supriyadi et al., 2020). This finding suggests that
applications improve accuracy primarily when paired with educational scaffolding, not as standalone tools. The
high accuracy of digital takhrij tools like Maktabah Syamilah (91.4%) is encouraging, but Abdullah et al. found that
accessibility barriers prevent ordinary social media users from leveraging these tools effectively (Abdullah et al.,
2026).

AI-based approaches show a sharp divergence: specialized models trained on isnad data achieve 84–99.94%
accuracy (Azwar & Usman, 2025; Alghamdi et al., 2025), while general-purpose LLMs like ChatGPT perform at only
8% accuracy ( Abd et al., 2026). This gap underscores that domain-specific training is essential, and that non-
experts relying on general AI chatbots for hadith verification face substantial inaccuracy risk. The Rezwan corpus
development demonstrates that AI can achieve near-human accuracy in structured tasks like chain–text separation
(9.33/10) while struggling with semantic similarity detection (Asgari-Bidhendi et al., 2025), highlighting that even
advanced AI remains a complementary rather than autonomous tool.

Several limitations recur across the corpus. Many applications lack standardized validation methods, raising risks of
misinformation (    Hamid, 2024; Bahrudin et al., 2025). The absence of authentication methods developed for
specific user groups, particularly non-experts, remains a identified gap (Bahrudin et al., 2025). Digital information is
easily edited, making originality and accuracy questionable (       Istianah & Wahyuningsih, 2019), and the shift toward
digital learning erodes traditional teacher–sanad authority (Syukur et al., 2026). Scholars emphasize that AI must
function as a supportive tool under human oversight rather than as an autonomous authority (          Susanti et al.,
2025;          ʿ
         IsmaĪl et al., 2026).

5. Conclusion
The literature indicates that digital hadith applications partially improve verification accuracy for non-experts, but
only when paired with educational scaffolding, domain-specific training, and scholarly oversight. Specialized AI
models and well-designed takhrij tools achieve high accuracy in structured authentication tasks, yet general-
purpose chatbots perform poorly, and many commercial applications lack adequate verification mechanisms. The
evidence consistently shows that technology functions best as a complementary tool within classical hadith
scholarship rather than as a replacement for expert judgment.

The most striking gap is the near-absence of empirical studies directly measuring non-expert verification accuracy
using digital tools, as opposed to measuring tool accuracy in isolation (Bahrudin et al., 2025). Most studies evaluate
applications from a developer or scholar perspective, leaving user-side performance largely untested. Longitudinal
data on whether digital literacy gains persist over time is also absent.
